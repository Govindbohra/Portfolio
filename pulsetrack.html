PulseTrack

From a broken Power BI dashboard to a real-time sentiment intelligence platform

Act 1 - The Problem

Senior leaders were drowning in data and still couldn't make a decision

Picture a Monday morning standup. Sarah, a Regional Head for Europe, knows something is wrong - sentiment has been slipping in her territory for weeks. To find out why, she opens three Power BI tabs, cross-references a spreadsheet, calls the analytics team, and waits until Thursday for a custom report. By then, the problem has already escalated.

This was the daily reality for NetSec Global's Customer Strategy Heads, Regional Sales Leaders, and Account Managers. The existing Power BI dashboard had data - lots of it. But it had no direction, no hierarchy, no trust signal, and no path to action.

The dashboard was not a tool. It was an obstacle between a problem and a decision.
No early warning system
Regional sentiment problems escalated before leadership knew they existed. There was no way to catch a declining market before it became a crisis.
Fragmented data sources
No single source of truth. Analysts manually consolidated reports across platforms before any decision could begin.
Insight-heavy, action-light
Dashboards showed numbers but offered no next step. Leaders could see a low NPS but had no path for what to do about it.
Global view, no local context
Business heads needed country-level comparisons to allocate resources. Global averages hid the real story.
Visual overload and distrust
Clashing colors, inconsistent charts, no brand alignment. Users dreaded opening it - and when they did, they didn't trust what they saw.
Opaque sentiment scoring
Stakeholders couldn't trace how scores were calculated, leading to mistrust and hesitancy to act even when the data was correct.

Act 2 - Research

I needed to understand the problem before I could frame the solution

I ran two rounds of research in parallel - stakeholder interviews to understand the business reality, and user interviews to understand the day-to-day frustration.

Round 1 - Business Interviews 2 stakeholders - 3 days

Talking to decision-makers surfaced the business cost of the problem. Dashboards were full of data but never drove a single action. Sentiment scoring was treated as a black box - leaders couldn't explain the numbers to their teams, so they stopped using them.

Round 2 - User Interviews 7 users - 3 days

The user sessions revealed something the business interviews hadn't: the problem wasn't just strategic, it was emotional. People felt fatigued, confused, and isolated - doing analysis alone with tools that gave them no support. Three things stood out immediately.

User interview insights - 7 participants, 3 days. Fatigue, isolation, and lack of context were the recurring themes.

Finding 01
Visual overload caused fatigue
Strong colors and lack of hierarchy left users exhausted. People avoided the dashboard unless absolutely necessary.
Finding 02
No filters meant no ownership
Everyone saw the same undifferentiated wall of data. Users couldn't create a view relevant to their region or role.
Finding 03
No collaboration features
Users couldn't tag teammates or comment on findings. Shared analysis happened over email chains, not inside the tool.

Act 4 - Opportunities

Five specific things the research told us to fix

The research didn't just reveal problems - it pointed directly at the design directions that would solve them. I distilled everything into five prioritised opportunities before touching a single wireframe.

Opportunity 01
Make the dashboard feel lighter
Reduce density, surface the most critical metrics first, and let users find what they need in under 10 seconds.
Opportunity 02
Bring filters forward
Filters existed but were buried. Bring them into the primary layout so personalisation is a first-class action, not an afterthought.
Opportunity 03
Clarify sentiment icons
Users weren't sure what the icons meant at first glance. Clear labels and consistent colour coding would build trust immediately.
Opportunity 04
Build a unified visual language
Charts changed style by region, which confused users and eroded confidence. A single design system would fix this across the board.

Act 5 - The Process

8 weeks, structured to move fast without skipping validation

I ran the project as a four-phase sprint. The critical decision was to bring real users in at week four - early enough to catch direction problems before they became expensive to fix, but after I had a tangible prototype to test against.

Act 6 - Prototyping & Iterations

I went straight to high-fidelity - then let testing reshape it

Because we already had solid research data, I skipped low-fidelity sketches and moved directly to clickable, high-fidelity prototypes. This accelerated the design process and let stakeholders react to something real in the first review session - not a sketch that required imagination.

Iteration 1 - First Draft

The first pass focused on consolidating all sentiment data into a single view. We introduced the KPI card strip at the top and the interactive map below. Initial testing showed users could locate global numbers quickly - but couldn't understand what to do with them. The hierarchy was flat; everything looked equally important.

Iteration 1 - first draft screenshot showing the original dashboard layout before visual hierarchy was added.

Iteration 2 - Introducing Visual Hierarchy

Testing feedback from round one was clear: users needed to know what was critical at a glance, without scanning every card. I introduced colour-coded sentiment states (green / grey / amber / red), timestamp badges on every data block, and a bolded summary panel that surfaced the most important finding in plain language. Adoption of the map increased significantly.

Iteration 2 screenshot showing the improved visual hierarchy with colour-coded sentiment states and clearer summary panels.

Iteration 3 - Adding the Next Actions Panel

The most significant design shift in the project. Users understood what was happening - but still didn't know what to do. In round-three testing, participants consistently said "OK, so what now?" after reviewing the data. I added an AI-generated "Next Actions" section at the bottom of every view, with prioritised steps like "Escalate: Seattle-based finance client flagged as Critical." This transformed PulseTrack from a reporting tool into an action platform.

Iteration 3 screenshot showing the final dashboard with the Next Actions panel added for immediate decision support.

Act 8 - The Solution

PulseTrack - a platform that tells you what's happening and what to do next

The final design is built around one principle: every screen should reduce the time between a signal and a decision. Here are the five design choices that achieved that.

1. Colour-coded sentiment states

Satisfied (green), Passive (grey), Dissatisfied (amber), Critical (red). Applied consistently across KPI cards, the client table, and the map. A manager can assess the health of 1,682 clients at a glance - no reading required.

2. Interactive regional map

Replaced static charts with a live map showing client locations as colour-coded dots. Clicking any dot surfaces that client's sentiment score, last update, and direct action link. Regional heads can now spot geographic patterns that were invisible in tabular data.

3. Filterable client table

Five filter dimensions - Country, Region, Quarter, Client Tier, Sentiment - always visible above the table. No buried menus, no modal dialogs. Advanced users like Daniel can build a precise view in two clicks.

4. AI-generated summary + Next Actions panel

The single biggest shift from the original tool. Every view ends with a plain-language summary of what the data means, followed by a prioritised list of specific next actions: which client to escalate, which region needs outreach, which segment shows testimonial potential. The platform moved from passive report to active advisor.

5. Persistent data freshness signals

"Last updated: May 14, 2025" appears on every section. A small detail that restored trust - users stopped second-guessing whether the data was current and started acting on it.

Act 11 - Reflection

What I would do differently, and what I'll carry forward

The biggest shift in this project was not visual - it was strategic. The old dashboard gave people data. The new one gives them direction. That difference only becomes clear when you spend time with the people who use the tool every day.

Working as a solo designer on a compressed 8-week sprint meant I had to make quick calls about where to invest depth. I chose research and testing over polish, and it paid off. The usability issues caught in round two would have been expensive to fix post-handoff.

What I would do differently

I would push to involve analysts like Daniel earlier in the define phase - not just business stakeholders. Their day-to-day friction around data export and filtering was some of the most impactful work we did, and I almost missed it by starting with the executive layer.

I would also build a lightweight content strategy for the AI-generated summary section earlier. The copy voice matters as much as the UI pattern when you're asking users to trust a machine-generated recommendation.

Constraint is your friend. The requirement to be fast and simple forced better design than "build everything" ever could. Enterprise design isn't about adding features - it's about removing barriers.
